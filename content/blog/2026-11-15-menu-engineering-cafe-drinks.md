Menu engineering for café drinks means sorting every drink on your menu by two numbers: how many cups it sells and how much profit each cup leaves after ingredient cost. That gives four groups (stars, plowhorses, puzzles and dogs), and each group gets one clear action: protect, fix, promote or remove. A café can run the full exercise in an afternoon with three months of POS data and a recipe card for every drink.

## What is menu engineering for a café?

The method comes from restaurant management and works the same way for a drinks bar. You compare each drink against the menu average on two axes:

- **Popularity**: cups sold in the period.
- **Contribution margin**: menu price minus ingredient cost per cup (base, milk, cup, lid, garnish).

Above average on both is a star, below average on both is a dog. [Toast's menu engineering guide](https://pos.toasttab.com/blog/on-the-line/menu-engineering-matrix) sums up the actions: protect stars, fix plowhorses, give puzzles more visibility, and test one change on a dog before removing it.

## What data do you need before you start?

1. **Sales by item** for the last 90 days from your POS, excluding staff drinks and voids.
2. **A costed recipe card** for every drink: grams of base, millilitres of milk or water, cup size, toppings.
3. **Current menu prices**, net of VAT.

The recipe card is the step most cafés skip. Without fixed doses, the cost per cup is a guess. If your team scoops by eye, weigh the doses for a week first.

## How do you calculate it, step by step?

1. For each drink, calculate **ingredient cost per cup** from the recipe card.
2. Calculate **contribution margin**: net price minus ingredient cost.
3. Calculate the **average margin** and **average cups sold** across all drinks in the category.
4. Place each drink in a quadrant against those two averages.
5. Analyse hot, iced and blended drinks separately. Mixing them distorts the averages, because blended drinks usually sell at a higher price.

## What should you do with each group?

**Stars** (popular, high margin):

- Keep the recipe and price stable.
- Put them where the eye lands first on the board.
- Train every barista to make them to the same standard.

**Plowhorses** (popular, low margin):

- These carry the most money, so they are the first place to work, as [Loaded's analysis points out](https://www.loadedhub.com/resources/menu-engineering-matrix-restaurant).
- Check the dose against the card. Overdosing is the most common hidden cost.
- Review the price in small steps, or pair the drink with a higher margin add on.

**Puzzles** (high margin, low sales):

- Rename and redescribe them. A flavour guests recognise sells better than a technical name.
- Ask baristas to suggest them, and run a short tasting at the counter.
- Move them next to a star on the board.

**Dogs** (low margin, low sales):

- Try one change for four weeks.
- If nothing moves, remove the drink and free the shelf space and the base it uses.

## How many drinks should a café menu have?

There is no fixed number, but a common rule is [three to five options per category](https://therestaurantwarehouse.com/blogs/restaurant-equipment/coffee-shop-menu-ideas). Fewer items mean faster ordering at peak hours, shorter training and less stock tied up on shelves. Keep a slot for two or three seasonal drinks and rotate them each quarter, so the board feels new without growing.

## How do you cut ingredients without cutting choice?

Count ingredients, not drinks. A menu of 20 drinks built on 8 bases is easier to run than 15 drinks built on 15 bases. Examples from our own recipe standards:

- **One chocolate base, three drinks**: classic hot chocolate (35 g base, 200 ml milk, 8 oz), saffron hot chocolate (20 g chocolate base plus 20 g saffron karak chai base, 180 ml milk) and orange blossom hot chocolate (15 g chocolate base plus 15 g orange Raf base, 180 ml milk).
- **One chai base, two drinks**: [chai latte](/chai-latte) (25 g base, 150 ml milk, 8 oz) and a date masala chai (25 g base, 180 ml milk, 10 ml date syrup).
- **One matcha base, hot and iced**: 20 g with 200 ml steamed milk in an 8 oz cup, or 30 g with 230 ml milk and 100 g ice in a 12 oz cup.

Each extra drink built on a base you already stock adds menu choice without adding a new SKU to order, store and train on.

## How often should you repeat the analysis?

Every quarter, and after every seasonal launch. Compare the new matrix with the previous one: a star that slips into plowhorse usually means a cost or dose change, not a change in what guests want.

## FAQ

### What is a plowhorse on a café menu?

A drink that sells well but leaves a below average margin. It is usually the best place to start, because small fixes to dose or price affect many cups.

### Should I remove every dog from the menu?

Not automatically. Test one change, such as a new name or a counter tasting, for about four weeks. If sales or margin do not move, remove it.

### How long does menu engineering take for a small café?

About an afternoon once recipe cards and 90 days of sales data are ready. The recipe cards take the most time the first time round.
